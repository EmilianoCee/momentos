Common source, common gate and common drain

Ground one terminal of a transistor for the signal, drive a second and take the output from the third. Common source amplifies and inverts, common gate takes in a current at a low resistance, and the source follower copies a voltage. Drag the bias point along each stage's curve: the gain is its slope, and it lasts only while the transistor stays saturated.

What should click

The gain is a slope. For a small signal the curve is as good as its tangent at the bias point, and the tangent's slope is A_v. The small-signal formulas compute that slope without drawing the curve.

One transistor, three jobs. Input on the gate and output at the drain: an inverting voltage amplifier. Input on the source: the source moves all of V_GS, so the input looks like 1/g_m, a current buffer. Output at the source: it follows the gate one V_GS lower, a voltage buffer with a gain just under 1.

Swing ends where saturation ends. The output can move only while the transistor stays saturated. Cut-off on one side and triode on the other bound the swing; more R_D steepens the curve but moves the bias toward triode.

The ceiling is g_m·r_o. With λ > 0, even an infinite R_D gives only the intrinsic gain g_m·r_o.

Predict, then try

  • Common source at its start. Double R_D. Does the gain double?
    whyNearly: it goes from −6.57 to −11.2, short of double because r_o is in parallel. But the drain drops from 1.93 V to 1.02 V, and the room below shrinks from 1.44 V to 0.66 V.
  • Now make R_D 40 kΩ. What happens to the gain?
    whyIt collapses to about −1. The same current would drop more than V_DD, so the drain falls into triode, where the transistor is no longer a current source. Gain from R_D only works while the stage stays saturated.
  • Switch to common gate. Which way does the output move when the input rises?
    whyUp. Raising the source lowers V_GS, so less current flows and R_D drops less: the gain is positive, about +7.7.
  • In common gate, what does the input source see?
    whyAbout 1/g_m, here 1.30 kΩ: the source moves the whole V_GS, so a small input voltage changes the current a lot. That low input resistance is why the stage is a current buffer.
  • Switch to the follower. Why is the gain below 1?
    whyThe output is the source, which has to stay one V_GS below the gate; for v_GS to change at all, the source must lag the gate a little. A_v = g_m R/(1 + g_m R), here 0.85.
  • Set λ = 0 in common source. What sets the ceiling on the gain now?
    whyNothing in the transistor: r_o is infinite and A_v = −g_m R_D. With λ back, the ceiling is the intrinsic gain g_m·r_o, reached only as R_D → ∞, where the swing is gone.

Where this shows up

  • common source: the inverting voltage amplifier, gain −g_m (r_o ∥ R_D).
  • common gate: the input at the source, R_in ≈ 1/g_m: a current buffer.
  • source follower: the output at the source, gain just under 1 and R_out ≈ 1/g_m: a voltage buffer.
  • output swing: the swing ends where the transistor leaves saturation, at cut-off or at the edge of triode.
  • intrinsic gain: g_m·r_o, the most one transistor gives.
  • small-signal analysis: each stage's A_v, R_in and R_out come from the same model at the bias point.
  • resistance looking in: the 1/g_m into a source and r_o ∥ R_D into a drain are the rules this tool shows at work.
