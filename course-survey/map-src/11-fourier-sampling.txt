# EE 120, part 2: Fourier series and transforms, sampling, modulation.

F signals/fourier
M fourier-series
T dtfs | discrete-time Fourier series | A sequence that repeats every N samples is a sum of exactly N phasors e^(j2πkn/N), for k = 0 to N − 1. N numbers in time become N numbers in frequency, and nothing is lost. | A sequence has period 4. How many distinct harmonics does it have, and why are there no more?
T dft | DFT | The DFT is that same change of basis applied to any N samples, written as an N by N matrix whose columns are orthogonal phasors. Its inverse is its conjugate transpose, up to a constant. | Why is the inverse of the DFT matrix so easy to write down?
T fft | FFT | The FFT computes the DFT by splitting the samples into even and odd halves, again and again. The work drops from N² operations to N log₂ N. | For N = 1024, roughly how many times less work is the FFT than the direct sum?
T fourier-symmetry | symmetry and shifts in Fourier series | The look of a signal in time fixes the pattern of its coefficients. A real signal has conjugate-symmetric coefficients, a real even signal has real ones, and a time shift only turns each coefficient's phase. | A real periodic signal is shifted by half a period. What happens to the magnitude and to the phase of its kth coefficient?
T parseval | Parseval's relation | The energy of a signal added up over time equals its energy added up over frequency. Changing to an orthogonal basis does not change a vector's length. | A periodic signal has only two non-zero Fourier coefficients, of magnitudes 3 and 4. What is its average power?
T fourier-transform | Fourier transform | Let the period grow without limit and the harmonics crowd together into a continuum. X(jω) says how much of the phasor e^(jωt) the signal contains, per unit of frequency. | A pulse is made narrower. Before any calculation, what must happen to its transform?
T dtft | DTFT | The DTFT is the spectrum of a sequence. It always repeats every 2π in ω, because a sequence cannot tell ω from ω + 2π. | x[n] is 1 at n = 0 and at n = 1, and zero elsewhere. Between −π and π, where is |X| largest and where is it zero?
T ft-time-shift | time shift is a phase ramp | Delay a signal and the magnitude of its transform does not change. Each frequency's phase turns by −ωt₀, a straight line in ω. | Two signals have the same |X(jω)| and different phases. What is the simplest way they can differ in time?
T ft-frequency-shift | frequency shift | Multiply a signal by e^(jω₀t) and its whole spectrum slides to ω₀. Multiplying by a cosine makes two half-size copies, at +ω₀ and −ω₀. | A signal whose spectrum is 1 kHz wide about zero is multiplied by a 10 kHz cosine. Where is its spectrum now?
T ft-convolution-property | convolution becomes multiplication | Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency. That is why filtering is simple in frequency: the output spectrum is the input spectrum times H. | A signal goes through the same filter twice. What happens to its spectrum?
T ft-multiplication-property | multiplication becomes convolution | Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency. Cutting a signal off with a window smears its spectrum by the window's transform. | A pure tone is observed for only 10 ms. Why is its measured spectrum not a single line, and roughly how wide is it?
T ft-duality | duality | The transform and its inverse have the same form, so every pair works both ways. A rectangle in time is a sinc in frequency, and a sinc in time is a rectangle in frequency. | The transform of a rectangle is a sinc. What is the transform of a sinc, and which familiar system has that frequency response?
T time-bandwidth | time and bandwidth trade off | Squeeze a signal in time and its spectrum spreads by the same factor. A signal cannot be short in time and narrow in frequency at once. | A pulse is made 10 times shorter. What happens to the bandwidth it needs?
T ft-differentiation | differentiation in frequency | Differentiating in time multiplies the transform by jω. High frequencies are boosted and a constant is removed. | Why does differentiating a noisy measurement make the noise look worse?
T spectrum-of-periodic-signals | spectrum of a periodic signal | A periodic signal's transform is a row of impulses at the harmonics, weighted by the Fourier series coefficients. Repeating in time is sampling in frequency. | One pulse has a smooth spectrum. The pulse is now repeated every T seconds. What does the spectrum become?
T impulse-train | impulse train | A row of impulses T apart in time transforms into a row of impulses 2π/T apart in frequency. Pack them closer in time and they move apart in frequency. | The impulses are moved twice as close together in time. What happens to the spacing of the lines in frequency?
T rect-and-sinc | rectangle and sinc | A rectangle of width T has a sinc for its transform, with its first zeros at ±2π/T. The narrower the rectangle, the wider the main lobe. | A rectangle is 2 ms wide. At what frequency, in hertz, is the first zero of its transform?
T windowing | windowing and leakage | Looking at a signal through a finite window replaces each spectral line by a copy of the window's transform. Its width blurs nearby tones together, and its side lobes leak energy across the spectrum. | Two tones 5 Hz apart are recorded for 0.1 s. Can the spectrum show two peaks? What would you change?
T fourier-transform-2d | Fourier transform in two dimensions | An image is a sum of two-dimensional waves, each with a direction and a spacing. A point of the 2-D transform far from the centre is fine detail in that direction. | An image contains vertical stripes only. Where in the 2-D frequency plane is its transform non-zero?

L fourier-transform > rect-and-sinc | The rectangle is the first transform worth knowing by heart, and almost every other pair is built from it.
L rect-and-sinc > ideal-filters | The ideal low-pass filter is a rectangle in frequency, so its impulse response is a sinc.
L fourier-series > spectrum-of-periodic-signals | The series coefficients become the weights of impulses at the harmonics.
L impulse-train > spectrum-of-periodic-signals | A periodic signal is one period convolved with an impulse train, so its spectrum is the period's spectrum sampled.

L ft-multiplication-property > windowing | Multiplying by a window convolves the spectrum with the window's transform.
L rect-and-sinc > windowing | The simplest window is a rectangle, so each line becomes a sinc.

F signals/sampling
T sampling-theorem | sampling theorem | Sampling every T seconds copies the spectrum at every multiple of the sampling frequency. If the copies do not overlap, the original is still there, so sample faster than twice the highest frequency. | A signal has nothing above 3 kHz. What is the slowest sampling rate that loses nothing, and what goes wrong just below it?
T aliasing | aliasing | When the copies overlap, a frequency above half the sampling rate lands on top of a lower one and cannot be told apart from it afterwards. | A 7 kHz tone is sampled at 10 kHz. What frequency does it appear as?
T reconstruction | reconstruction | To get the signal back, keep one copy of the spectrum with a low-pass filter. In time, that is placing a sinc on every sample and adding them up. | Between two samples, what decides the value of the rebuilt signal: its two neighbours only, or all the samples?
T zero-order-hold | zero-order hold | A practical converter holds each sample until the next one, making a staircase. Holding is itself a filter: it droops the top of the band and leaves faint images above it. | What does the staircase contain that the smooth original does not, and where is it in frequency?
T dt-processing-of-ct | digital filtering of analog signals | Sample, filter the numbers, then reconstruct. Below half the sampling rate the whole chain acts as one continuous-time filter: the digital filter's response with its frequency axis stretched by the sampling rate. | A digital low-pass has its cutoff at π/4 rad per sample, and the sampling rate is 8 kHz. What is the cutoff of the whole chain in hertz?
T downsampling | downsampling | Keep every Mth sample and the spectrum stretches by M. Anything above π/M beforehand folds back on top of the rest, so filter first. | A sequence with content up to π/2 is downsampled by 3 with no filter. Does it alias? Why?
T upsampling | upsampling | Insert M − 1 zeros between samples and the spectrum squeezes by M, which brings M − 1 unwanted images into view. A low-pass filter removes them, and in time it fills in the zeros. | One zero is inserted between each pair of samples. Where does the unwanted image sit before filtering?
L impulse-train > sampling-theorem | Sampling is multiplying by an impulse train, which convolves the spectrum with another impulse train.
L ft-multiplication-property > sampling-theorem | Multiplying in time by the sampling train is convolving in frequency, which makes the copies.
L sampling-theorem > aliasing | Aliasing is what the overlapping copies do.
L dt-frequency > aliasing | Seen from the samples, a frequency and its alias are the same sequence.
L sampling-theorem > reconstruction | If the copies do not overlap, a low-pass filter can keep one of them.
L ideal-filters > reconstruction | The filter that keeps exactly one copy is the ideal low-pass.
L rect-and-sinc > reconstruction | That filter's impulse response is the sinc placed on each sample.
L reconstruction > zero-order-hold | Holding each sample is the crude interpolation that real converters use in place of the sinc.
L sampling-theorem > dt-processing-of-ct | With no aliasing, the samples carry the whole signal, so filtering the samples filters the signal.
L dt-frequency-response > dt-processing-of-ct | The digital filter's response becomes the response of the whole chain.
L aliasing > downsampling | Keeping every Mth sample is sampling again, and it aliases for the same reason.
L dtft > downsampling | The effect is read off the DTFT: stretched by M and added to its shifted copies.
L dtft > upsampling | Inserting zeros squeezes the DTFT by M.
L reconstruction > upsampling | The low-pass filter after the zeros is interpolation, the discrete version of reconstruction.

T amplitude-modulation | amplitude modulation | Multiply a message by a carrier cos(ω_c t) and its spectrum moves from around zero to around ±ω_c, where an antenna or a shared cable can carry it. | A message 4 kHz wide is modulated onto a 100 kHz carrier. Which band of frequencies does the result occupy?
T synchronous-demodulation | synchronous demodulation | Multiply the received signal by the same carrier again and low-pass filter: one copy of the message lands back at zero frequency. A phase error φ in the local carrier scales the result by cos φ. | The receiver's carrier is 90° out of phase with the transmitter's. What comes out of the demodulator?
T quadrature-modulation | quadrature modulation | A cosine carrier and a sine carrier at the same frequency are orthogonal, so two messages can share one band and still be separated. | Two messages ride on cosine and sine carriers of the same frequency. What does the receiver multiply by to get each one alone, and what must be accurate for that to work?
T frequency-division-multiplexing | frequency-division multiplexing | Give each message its own carrier frequency and the spectra sit side by side without overlapping. A band-pass filter picks one out. | Ten messages, each 4 kHz wide, share a cable using ordinary AM. What is the least total bandwidth they need?
L ft-frequency-shift > amplitude-modulation | Multiplying by a cosine is the frequency-shift property used twice, once up and once down.
L amplitude-modulation > synchronous-demodulation | Shifting again by the same frequency puts one copy back at zero and another at twice the carrier.
L filter-types > synchronous-demodulation | A low-pass filter keeps the copy at zero and removes the one at twice the carrier.
L amplitude-modulation > quadrature-modulation | It is amplitude modulation done twice in the same band, on two carriers.
L signal-inner-product > quadrature-modulation | Cosine and sine at the same frequency have zero inner product over a period, which is what keeps the messages apart.
L amplitude-modulation > frequency-division-multiplexing | Each message is moved to its own carrier.
L filter-types > frequency-division-multiplexing | A band-pass filter selects one of the side-by-side spectra.
