# How mastery is built here

The map, the tools, the quizzes and past-exam practice are parts of one loop. This page says what each part is
for, and why the loop has the shape it has. Each choice rests on a finding from research on learning, cited at
the end. The summaries are ours and are kept modest: read the paper before leaning on a detail.

## The loop

| Step | What you do | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Find the level | get placed on the ladder for one topic, from evidence | your record, the `onboard` skill, the first questions of a quiz |
| 2. Build the picture | see the idea move, predict before you look, explain it back | the map's `essence`, a tool's "Predict, then try", a teaching session |
| 3. Practise | solve standard problems until the procedure is reliable | homework, a tool's practice mode, `compute` questions |
| 4. Retrieve | answer from memory, with no notes and no hints | `quiz me`, the map's `check` questions |
| 5. Space it out | come back after days, then weeks | `review`, the dates in your record |
| 6. Transfer | use the idea on a problem you have not seen, and say where it breaks | `transfer` and `limit` questions, past exams, `exam prep` |

Steps 4 to 6 are where a level is earned. Steps 2 and 3 make them possible.

## Why each step is there

**1. The level decides how to teach.** Guidance that helps a beginner gets in the way of someone who already has
the procedure: worked examples speed up a novice and slow down an expert. This is the expertise reversal effect
(Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller, 2003). So a session first finds the level for the topic, and the level
picks the mode: the session leads at L0 to L1, works a problem with you at L2 to L3, and follows your lead at L4
to L5. A level is kept per topic because expertise is per topic.

**2. Predict before you look, and explain it back.** Students who predicted the outcome of a physics
demonstration before seeing it understood it better afterwards than students who only watched (Crouch, Fagen,
Callan and Mazur, 2004). So every tool has "Predict, then try" prompts with the answers folded away, and
prediction is the gate to L3. Students who explained worked examples to themselves, step by step, learned more
from them than students who read them (Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann and Glaser, 1989). So `explain` and `sketch`
questions ask for the reasoning in your own words, and L3 needs one of them.

**3. Mix the practice.** When practice problems of different kinds are shuffled together instead of grouped by
kind, performance during practice is worse and performance on a later test is better (Rohrer and Taylor, 2007;
Rohrer, Dedrick and Stershic, 2015). Part of the skill is recognising which idea a problem calls for, and blocked
practice never asks for that. So a quiz draws neighbouring questions from different areas, and a `transfer`
problem never names its method.

**4. Retrieval is the main activity, not the test at the end.** Being tested on material produces better
retention after a delay than studying it again for the same time, even though restudying feels more effective
(Roediger and Karpicke, 2006), and retrieval practice outperformed an elaborate study method, concept mapping
(Karpicke and Blunt, 2011). So the centre of this system is the quiz. Questions are written fresh, no hint comes
before an answer, and an answer given after a hint is recorded but cannot raise a level.

**5. Space it out, and widen the gaps.** Practice spread over time is remembered better than the same practice
done at once (Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer, 2006), and the gap that works best grows with how long the
memory has to last (Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted and Pashler, 2008). Recalling to a criterion once is not enough:
relearning the same material in later, spaced sessions is what makes it durable (Rawson and Dunlosky, 2011). So L3
cannot be earned in one day, every level from L2 up has a next review, and passed reviews move 3, 7, 14, 30, 60
and 120 days apart. The intervals are a sensible default, not an optimum. Before an exam, `exam prep` ignores them.

**6. Transfer has to be practised as transfer.** Using what was learned in a situation unlike the one it was
learned in is hard, and it gets harder the further the new situation is in content and in context (Barnett and
Ceci, 2002). So L4 is not "L3 with more practice". It needs a problem the learner must work out how to approach,
solved without help, and it needs the learner to say where the model stops being true. Past exams belong here:
work them closed-book and timed, then ask for `transfer` questions on the topics that went badly.

**Calibration runs through all of it.** Students who judged their own wrong answers to be right stopped studying
too soon and remembered less (Dunlosky and Rawson, 2012). So a self-rating never counts for more than L1, you
state a confidence before each answer, and the report shows confidence against results.

**It is meant to feel harder than rereading.** Spacing, mixing and testing make practice slower and less
comfortable while improving what lasts. Conditions that make practice feel fluent can leave little behind (Bjork
and Bjork, 2011). A broad review of study techniques rated practice testing and distributed practice the most
useful, and rereading and highlighting among the least (Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham, 2013).

## What this does not claim

- A level is an estimate from a handful of answers. It can be wrong in both directions. The fall-back and win-back
  rules exist so that it corrects itself.
- The requirements per level and the review intervals were chosen by judgement, guided by the findings above. They
  were not tuned on data. When they change, every level is recomputed from the same evidence.
- Most of the research was done on verbal material and school mathematics. It is applied here to engineering
  intuition by analogy.
