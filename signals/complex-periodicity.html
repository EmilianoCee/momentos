Periodicity, by going round in circles

Multiplying by j turns a point a quarter of the way round the origin. Multiplying by ejΩ turns it by the angle Ω. Keep multiplying and the point walks round the unit circle in equal steps. The signal repeats exactly when that walk comes back to where it began.

What should click

Multiplying is turning. Every number on the unit circle is ejΩ = cos Ω + j sin Ω for some angle Ω, and multiplying by it turns a point through that angle without changing its distance from the origin. So ejΩn is simply where you are after n equal turns. With Ω a quarter turn the multiplier is j itself, and j4 = 1 is the smallest example of something that repeats.

Periodic means the walk closes. The sequence repeats when some whole number of steps N lands exactly on the starting point: NΩ = 2πk for whole numbers N and k. That is only possible when Ω is a fraction k/N of a full turn. Then the period is the denominator N, and k counts how many times the point goes round before it repeats. One radian per step is not such a fraction, because π is irrational, so that walk never closes.

A whole turn is invisible. Step by 9/8 of a turn and you land exactly where 1/8 of a turn would have put you. Looking only at the landing points you cannot tell Ω from Ω + 2π, and 7/8 of a turn forwards is indistinguishable from 1/8 of a turn backwards. That is all aliasing is.

Turning smoothly always closes. Let the angle grow continuously, ejωt, and it passes through every value on the way, so it always completes a turn: after T = 2π/ω. Cosine and sine are the two shadows of that one turning point, which is why they repeat together, a quarter turn apart.

Why carry the imaginary part at all. A cosine on its own cannot say which way the point is turning. The pair of shadows, kept together as one complex number, can. And moving forward in time becomes one more multiplication, which is far easier to work with than trigonometric identities.

Predict, then try


  • How many steps until the point is back at 1?
    whyFour. Each step is a quarter turn: 1, j, −1, −j, and then 1 again.

  • It can only ever land on eight points. How many times does it go round before it repeats?
    whyThree. Eight steps of 3/8 of a turn make exactly 3 turns, and no smaller number of steps makes a whole number.

  • More than a full turn per step. How will the samples differ from 1/8?
    whyThey do not differ at all. Only the faint path between the steps shows the extra turn.

  • Which way does the point seem to be going?
    whyBackwards, an eighth of a turn at a time. The cosine samples match those of 1/8 and the sine samples are flipped.

  • Will it ever land exactly on 1 again?
    whyNo. It comes close at step 44, because 44 radians is nearly 7 full turns (22/7 ≈ π), and closer still at step 333. Close is not the same as closed.

  • Is there any speed at which this does not repeat?
    whyOnly zero. Anything else completes a turn after 1/f seconds.

Where this shows up

  • Phasors: every voltage in a circuit driven at one frequency turns at the same rate, so you keep only where each one starts.
  • Fourier series: a periodic signal is a sum of these turning points, making 1, 2, 3, … turns per period.
  • Frequency response: put ejωt into a linear time-invariant system and the same turning point comes out, scaled and shifted.
  • Euler's formula is the statement that the two shadows are cosine and sine.
